Run 2: VICReg rebalanced + lr_encoder fix — phase 1 healed, phase 2 drift persists

Date 2026-06-04 Component Price Encoder / VICReg / Training Loop Status Inconclusive Time ~2 h (full 2000-step run)

Summary

Run 2 applied two fixes from the previous entry: a dedicated lr_encoder = 1e-4 for the from-scratch price encoder (own optimizer group, separate from the LoRA adapters) and a rebalanced VICReg (λ_v = 10, λ_c = 0.05, down from 25/1.0). Both fixes worked as intended: phase 1 is now healthy (val_jepa starts at 0.044, down from 0.066), and the covariance term no longer dominates (end-of-run: λ_c·c ≈ 0.13 ≈ jepa ≈ 0.13 — roughly equal contributions). But val_jepa still rises monotonically, 0.044 → 0.147 over 2000 steps, while val z_std stays 0.58–0.76 against a train z_std of ~1.05. The VICReg domination problem is solved; the new bottleneck is phase-2 encoder drift: the encoder, now actually learning at 1e-4, shifts training representations faster than ema_decay = 0.998 can smooth, the predictor overfits to the drifted train distribution, and val held-out representations land in a different region.

Hypothesis / Motivation

Run 1 was diagnosed as: (a) VICReg covariance dominating the loss and leaving only ~4% budget for the JEPA objective, and (b) the from-scratch encoder stuck at the LoRA rate (3e-5) and barely moving. Run 2 tested both fixes simultaneously. Expectation: phase-1 projection warmup would still converge (the projection-only phase is not affected by VICReg weights), and phase-2 would now see a healthy gradient split (JEPA ≈ VICReg) with an encoder that actually learns. The question was whether those two changes were sufficient to reverse the val_jepa trend.

Analysis & Findings

What the VICReg fix accomplished

The rebalance worked exactly as intended. In Run 1, λ_c·c ≈ 1.73 vs jepa ≈ 0.08 — the optimizer was spending ~95% of its effective gradient budget on decorrelation. Now λ_c·c ≈ 0.13 ≈ jepa ≈ 0.13: prediction and covariance regularisation are roughly equal weight. Phase 1 also improved: val_jepa at step 250 dropped from 0.066 (Run 1) to 0.044 (Run 2) — a 33% improvement, consistent with projections now spending more gradient on JEPA rather than fighting a too-large covariance penalty.

The new bottleneck: phase-2 encoder drift

The encoder LR fix succeeded at making the encoder learn, but learning is not the same as generalising. The mechanism:

  1. Phase 1 runs 500 steps with the encoder frozen at random init. The projection heads learn to predict EMA-target outputs given random encoder features — essentially learning to match random projections, a degenerate but easy task.
  2. Phase 2 activates lr_encoder = 1e-4 with a 100-step warmup. The encoder escapes its random-init basin and starts producing temporally-structured features.
  3. As encoder representations shift, the EMA target (decay = 0.998 ≈ 200-step averaging) follows with modest lag. The predictor adapts its predictions to the new representation space on the training distribution.
  4. Val representations come from a later time period (chronological split). The encoder's phase-2 drift pushes training-period representations in a direction that doesn't align with the held-out period, so val_jepa rises as the encoder specialises to the train distribution.

The val z_std gap (0.58–0.76 vs train 1.05) is the smoking gun: the variance hinge is satisfied on training batches but doesn't generalise. Val embeddings are partially collapsed, meaning the encoder is producing less diverse representations on out-of-sample time periods — consistent with overfitting to the temporal patterns in the training window.

VICReg balance confirmed: λ_c·c ≈ JEPA at end of run. Phase 1 val_jepa improved from 0.066 → 0.044. Both fixes from the previous entry worked as intended.
Val z_std recovers slightly toward the end (0.582 at step 750 → 0.762 at step 2000), suggesting the encoder is slowly generalising as it settles. Whether longer training would see val_jepa turn over is unknown — a 4000-step run might be worth trying alongside the LR/EMA changes.
Phase-2 encoder drift is the binding failure: lr_encoder=1e-4 on ema_decay=0.998 drifts representations faster than the target can smooth, and the predictor overfits to the shifted train distribution. This must be addressed before val_jepa can improve.

Thought Process & Debugging Notes

Initial read of the logs: "val_jepa rises again, something is still wrong." First instinct was to check whether the VICReg rebalance actually took effect — verified by decomposing the final loss, and yes it did. So the covariance explanation from Run 1 was right but incomplete: it was one problem, not the only problem.

Second read: val_jepa starts better (0.044 vs 0.066) and the trajectory only turns upward after Phase 2 begins (step 500). Everything in Phase 1 is fine. So the failure is specifically triggered by the encoder unlocking. That rules out data issues in Phase 1 and points squarely at what happens when lr_encoder activates.

The val z_std gap pattern is the key: train is pinned at ~1.05 (variance hinge working) while val floats between 0.58–0.76. This is the classic "regulariser satisfied on training distribution, ignored on test distribution" pattern. In a standard supervised model this would mean the model overfits; in a self-supervised representation model it means the encoder learns train-specific variance patterns that don't hold on val.

Two levers: slow down the encoder (lower LR) or slow down the target (higher EMA decay). They attack the same problem from different directions. The third lever — starting the encoder earlier so there's no phase discontinuity — is more structural and requires a trainer change, but is worth noting as the principled long-term fix.

Lessons Learned

  • "The encoder is now learning" is not the same as "the encoder is learning useful generalisable representations." Verify on val, not just by watching loss terms move.
  • Monotonically rising val_jepa with a simultaneously rising train_jepa means the problem is not pure overfitting (train would fall while val rose) — it means the whole optimisation trajectory is moving away from good representations on both sets, just slower on val than on train when drift is the cause.
  • EMA decay of 0.998 is a 500-step effective window (~1/(1−0.998)). With a 100-step LR warmup and lr=1e-4, the encoder can substantially shift representations within half of that window — the EMA doesn't smooth fast enough relative to the encoder LR.
  • Phase 1 (projections trained on random-encoder outputs) creates a warm-start that disappears the moment the encoder escapes its random-init basin. The phase discontinuity may be the structural source of the drift problem for from-scratch encoders.

Next Steps

  1. Run 3 — implemented: reduce lr_encoder: 1e-4 → 5e-5 and increase ema_decay: 0.998 → 0.999. These attack the drift from both sides — slower encoder movement and a longer effective EMA window (~1000 steps at 0.999 vs ~500 at 0.998). Pick the smaller encoder LR as the conservative first step; if val_jepa still rises, try 3e-5 next, or address the phase-structure issue instead.
  2. Structural fix (requires trainer change): train the encoder from Phase 1 alongside the projections, at a lower rate (e.g. 5e-6 in Phase 1, 5e-5 in Phase 2). This eliminates the random-init discontinuity at step 500 and lets the predictor co-adapt with the encoder from the start. The LoRA adapters can still stay frozen until Phase 2.
  3. If val_jepa still rises after Run 3: run a PCA/UMAP of val z_price to check whether the temporal drift is structural (train and val embeddings live in different regions because they span different market regimes) or incidental (the encoder learned the right structure but wrong scale). The former requires more data diversity; the latter might be fixed by normalising z_price at inference.
  4. Consider extending max_steps to 4000 — the val z_std recovery trend (0.58 at step 750 → 0.76 at step 2000) suggests the encoder may eventually stabilise; a longer run might let it do so before the LR decays to near zero.
