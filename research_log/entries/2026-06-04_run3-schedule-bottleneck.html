Run 3: val_jepa turns over — but the cosine schedule hit the floor first

Date 2026-06-04 Component Price Encoder / Training Loop / LR Schedule Status Inconclusive Time ~3 h (2000-step run to completion)

Summary

Run 3 applied the two fixes from Run 2's diagnosis: lr_encoder 1e-4 → 5e-5 (halve encoder drift) and ema_decay 0.998 → 0.999 (double the effective EMA target window from ~500 to ~1000 steps). Both changes had their intended effect: the previously monotonically-rising val_jepa stopped rising uniformly, and — for the first time across three runs — decreased between the final two checkpoints (0.0953 at step 1750 → 0.0945 at step 2000). Held-out representation diversity (val z_std) recovered monotonically through the second half of training (0.623 at step 750 → 0.749 at step 2000), which had never happened before.

However, the model was clearly still improving when training ended. Inspecting the LR trace reveals why: by step 2000, the cosine schedule had already decayed lr_enc to its minimum — 5e-6, exactly 10% of peak — because the 2000-step total budget gave only 1400 steps of phase-2 cosine decay (steps 600–2000). The encoder had barely started learning at a useful rate before the schedule pulled it back to near-zero. The diagnosis: not the wrong learning rate, but a schedule too short to let that rate do any meaningful work. Run 4 extends to 5000 steps (4400 steps of cosine decay), restores lr_encoder = 1e-4, and bumps lr_adapter = 5e-5.

Root Cause: The Cosine Schedule Hit the Floor

The key diagnostic is in the lr_enc column. By step 2000, it reads exactly 5e-6 — precisely lr_min_factor × lr_encoder = 0.1 × 5e-5. The schedule had nothing left to give. The encoder spent the last ~400 steps of training essentially frozen at the minimum rate, and val_jepa only turned over in that final window. What we observed at step 2000 is not the model converging — it is the model finally reaching a low-enough drift rate that val representations could catch up, but at a point where the LR was too low to sustain the improvement.

How the cosine schedule works in this codebase

-- phase-2 cosine schedule for any param group -- lr_peak = lr_encoder (or lr_adapter) lr_min = lr_min_factor × lr_peak = 0.1 × peak t_start = warmup_proj_steps + lr_warmup_steps = 500 + 100 = 600 T = max_steps - t_start = total cosine decay length at step s (after warmup): progress = (s - t_start) / T ∈ [0, 1] lr(s) = lr_min + (lr_peak - lr_min) × 0.5 × (1 + cos(π × progress))
Show numerical derivation for each run
Run 3 (max_steps = 2000)
T = 2000 - 600 = 1400 steps at step 2000 (progress = 1.0): lr(2000) = 5e-6 + 0 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(π)) = 5e-6 + 0 = 5e-6 ← floor at step 1750 (progress = 0.821): lr(1750) = 5e-6 + 45e-6 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(0.821π)) = 5e-6 + 45e-6 × 0.5 × (1 − 0.643) = 5e-6 + 45e-6 × 0.179 ≈ 5e-6 + 8.0e-6 = 13e-6 90% of decay complete by step 1890.
Run 4 (max_steps = 5000, lr_peak = 1e-4)
T = 5000 - 600 = 4400 steps at "old step 2000" (progress = 1400/4400 = 0.318): lr(2000) = 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(0.318π)) = 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.5 × (1 + 0.540) = 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.770 ≈ 1e-5 + 6.93e-5 = 7.93e-5 at step 3000 (progress = 0.545): lr(3000) ≈ 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(0.545π)) ≈ 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.5 × (1 − 0.176) ≈ 1e-5 + 9e-5 × 0.412 ≈ 4.7e-5

At the point where Run 3 hit its floor (5e-6), Run 4's encoder will still be running at 7.93e-5 — 15.9× higher. Same peak LR as Run 2 (1e-4), but spread over 4400 decay steps instead of 1400. Run 2 was too fast (1e-4 concentrated into 1400 steps = aggressive drift). Run 4 is the same peak distributed over 3× more steps = gradual, sustained training.

The insight: Peak learning rate and schedule length interact multiplicatively. "Aggressive training" doesn't mean just raising the LR — it means having enough high-LR steps to actually move the parameters. Run 3 had 1400 effective steps at a 5e-5 peak. Run 4 has 4400 steps at a 1e-4 peak — roughly 6× the cumulative gradient magnitude over phase 2, even accounting for the cosine decay shape.

What val z_std is telling us

val z_std is the per-dimension standard deviation of held-out z_price embeddings, averaged over dimensions. The VICReg variance hinge pushes this toward gamma = 1.0 on training batches only. If the encoder learns structure that genuinely separates different market regimes, val z_std should naturally approach 1.0 as well — because the diversity that exists in the training set also exists in the validation set. If the encoder is overfitting, val z_std diverges downward (collapsed, non-diverse val representations).

The dip-then-recovery pattern

val z_std dips to its minimum at step 750 (0.623), right after the encoder activates and starts moving representations. This is not a failure — it is the encoder escaping its random-init basin and disrupting the "easy prediction on random features" equilibrium established in phase 1. Once the encoder settles into a new, more structured embedding space, val z_std recovers.

In Run 2 (lr_enc=1e-4), the dip went to 0.582 and the recovery was incomplete (stuck at 0.762 by step 2000, with val_jepa still rising fast). The encoder moved too fast for the EMA to smooth, so the "settling" never happened — it kept drifting.

In Run 3 (lr_enc=5e-5, ema_decay=0.999), the dip is shallower (0.623) and the recovery is monotone. val z_std at step 2000 (0.749) is close to Run 2's (0.762) despite a slower encoder — meaning more of the diversity is genuine and less is "inflated by the training hinge".

Analysis & Findings

The three runs tell a coherent story

Looking across all three runs, there is a single underlying quantity that explains every outcome: how many steps does the encoder train at a meaningfully high LR?

Run 1: encoder learned too slowly, val_jepa plateaued (no representation change = no drift, but also no improvement). Run 2: encoder learned at the right magnitude but outpaced the EMA's ability to smooth the target, causing drift. Run 3: right magnitude (ema_decay=0.999 slowed the effective target update, halving the rate addressed the drift) but a schedule that expired before convergence. The val_jepa decrease at step 2000 is the clearest signal yet that 5e-5 is in the right regime — if only there were more steps left.

Why lr_encoder=1e-4 is safe in Run 4 but failed in Run 2

Run 2 had ema_decay=0.998, giving the EMA an effective window of ~500 steps (1 / (1 − 0.998)). With lr_enc=1e-4 and 1400 cosine steps, the encoder moved fast enough to substantially shift representations within that 500-step window. The predictor trained on the shifted distribution; val representations lagged behind; val_jepa rose.

Run 4 has ema_decay=0.999 (~1000-step window) and 4400 cosine steps. Even with lr_enc=1e-4, the change in representations per 1000-step EMA window is much smaller because the cosine schedule keeps the LR high but the gradient steps are being used more efficiently (the encoder isn't just thrashing in the first 1400 steps). It's the difference between sprinting 1400 meters and pacing yourself over 4400.

val_jepa decreased for the first time. val z_std recovered monotonically after step 750. The rate-of-increase deceleration (from +24% to −0.8% across checkpoints) is a clean trend, not noise. The encoder is learning the right things — it just needs more time.
The cosine schedule hit its floor by step 2000. The model was still improving at the maximum rate possible given the remaining LR (≈ zero). Running longer at 5e-5 floor is not useful — a meaningfully higher LR for the full 5000-step budget is what's needed.

Thought Process & Debugging Notes

First read of the logs: the val_jepa deceleration pattern is immediately visible — the percentage increases across checkpoints are shrinking. That alone suggests the Run 2 diagnosis was correct (slower drift) and the remaining issue is convergence speed, not fundamentals.

The lr_enc column is the smoking gun. At step 1750, it reads 8.48e-6, which is 17% of the 5e-5 peak. By step 2000 it's exactly 5e-6 — the theoretical minimum. This isn't approximately at the floor; it is the floor, computed as lr_min_factor × lr_encoder = 0.1 × 5e-5 exactly. So the model went from 8.48e-6 at step 1750 to 5e-6 at step 2000 — a period of near-zero learning — and val_jepa still managed to tick down. That's the most optimistic possible reading of a bad situation.

The user described the model as "untrained", which is exactly right in a specific sense: the encoder's cosine budget was exhausted before convergence. The difference between an "undertrained" model and a "converged-but-limited" model matters for what to try next. If it were the latter, more steps wouldn't help — you'd need more data or a different architecture. Since it's the former, extending the schedule should show clear continued improvement.

The decision to restore lr_encoder=1e-4 in Run 4 came from the math: with 4400 decay steps instead of 1400, the cosine curve is three times flatter. A 1e-4 encoder LR decayed over 4400 steps moves representations more slowly at any given step than the same LR over 1400 steps. Run 4 is not "more aggressive than Run 2 despite the same peak LR" — it is less aggressive per step, but more aggressive in total training effort. The ema_decay=0.999 window (~1000 steps) now comfortably spans the region of the schedule where LR is high, so the target gets to average over many near-peak-LR steps before the encoder moves substantially.

Lessons Learned

  • Read the LR trace, not just the loss. val_jepa turning over is good news, but the lr_enc column tells you why and whether it will continue. A model improving at near-zero LR is not converging — it is coasting on momentum.
  • Cosine schedule length and peak LR are not independent. The same peak LR over 3× more steps is a fundamentally different training regime — representations change more slowly per EMA window, making the target smoother. "Reduce peak LR to reduce drift" and "extend the schedule" are interchangeable to first order when EMA decay stays fixed.
  • val z_std's dip-then-recovery is a healthy pattern. The dip at step 750 is not failure — it is the encoder escaping its random-init basin and disrupting the degenerate phase-1 equilibrium. The question to ask is whether it recovers. In Run 3 it recovered monotonically. In Run 2 it partially recovered but val_jepa kept rising, because the encoder was moving too fast to settle.
  • Deceleration in val_jepa rate-of-increase is a leading indicator. The transitions +24% → +17% → +11% → +6% → −0.8% were visible well before the final decrease. If this deceleration appeared earlier and the LR budget hadn't run out, we would have seen the turnaround sooner.

Next Steps (Run 4)

  1. max_steps: 2000 → 5000. Primary change. Phase-2 cosine decay extends from 1400 to 4400 steps. At step 2000 (the old finish line), lr_enc will be ~7.9e-5 instead of 5e-6.
  2. lr_encoder: 5e-5 → 1e-4. Run 2 showed 1e-4 with 1400 decay steps drifts too fast. Run 3 showed 5e-5 with 1400 steps converges but runs out of schedule. Run 4: 1e-4 with 4400 steps. The cosine curve is ~3× flatter, the per-step representation change is similar to Run 3, but the model gets to run that change for 3× as long.
  3. lr_adapter: 3e-5 → 5e-5. The predictor LoRA is on the same cosine curve — it was also at its floor by step 2000. The predictor may be the limiting factor in val_jepa: if the encoder produces good representations but the predictor can't fit to them, val_jepa won't decrease. Bump it proportionally to the encoder rate increase.
  4. Watch for: val_jepa decreasing consistently across checkpoints in the 1000–3000 step window (not just the final step), val z_std approaching 0.85+, and the train/val JEPA gap narrowing. If val_jepa continues to rise even in Run 4, the data (regime diversity) is the likely explanation and a PCA/UMAP of val embeddings is the next diagnostic.
