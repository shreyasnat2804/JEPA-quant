nb04 Ceiling Probe: TimesFM refuted, raw moments win

Date 2026-06-06 Component Evaluation / Pretrained Backbone (TimesFM) / Input Pipeline Status Failed Time ~30 min (forward passes only, no training)

Summary

The Stage 1 follow-up (2026-06-05) left two faults on the table: the trained projection head destroys downstream signal, AND the frozen random backbone is itself weak (~0.08 vol R²). The proposed fix was a pretrained backbone (TimesFM). Before spending any training units, nb04 runs the cheap, decisive test: probe RAW TimesFM pooled features — no JEPA, no projection head, no training — against the same linear-probe harness, alongside the untrained random floor, the trained checkpoint, and a hand-crafted raw-input baseline. This is the ceiling any frozen-backbone JEPA inherits.

The pivot is refuted. On future volatility (the most decodable target), RAW TimesFM lands at val R² −0.134 — below the untrained random-projection floor (+0.097) and far below a trivial 4-moment baseline (+0.333) — while overfitting hard (train +0.246) with α pinned at the grid maximum. A frozen-TimesFM JEPA loop is not justified by this evidence.

The real headline is the floor, not the ceiling. A 24-dimensional [mean, std, last, sum]-per-channel baseline decodes future volatility at +0.333 val R², beating trained z_price (+0.118), the random projection (+0.097), and TimesFM (−0.134) by a wide margin. The volatility signal is real, strong, and trivially linear — no foundation model, no JEPA, needed. The project's bar must move to +0.33, not the +0.097 floor.

Hypothesis / Motivation

JEPA cannot manufacture downstream signal that is absent from the backbone's representation. With a frozen backbone its only encoder lever is the projection head, which the prior entry showed collapses. So the RAW-feature probe is the ceiling JEPA inherits — measuring it cleanly separates failure modes before any training units are spent.

Do RAW pretrained TimesFM features linearly decode our targets on val,
better than the random-projection floor and a raw-input baseline,
with NO JEPA / NO head / NO training?

H1: RAW TimesFM vol R² beats both the floor (by >0.03) and raw inputs (by >0.02) → signal exists that the project's loop destroys; a backbone swap is worth training. H0: TimesFM ≈ floor → a pretrained backbone alone is not the bottleneck. Falsifier: TimesFM vol R² within ~0.01 of (or below) the floor.

Experimental Setup

Four feature extractors — one identical harness

raw_input         : per-channel [mean, std, last, sum] of the normalized context  → D=24   (no encoder)
untrained_zprice  : random-init head + frozen random backbone (the Stage 1 FLOOR) → D=256
trained_zprice    : nb03_best post-head latent                                    → D=256
timesfm           : RAW TimesFM-2.0-500m pooled last_hidden_state, mean over patches, close channel → D=1280  (CEILING)

Every representation goes through the same probe_*_features(encode_fn, …) path (same cfg, n_train=100 batches, n_val=50, seed=42, WIDE α grid 0.01…1e4). The floor and trained baselines are re-run in-session under the wide grid, not cited from Stage 1 (which pinned α=100, its grid max). TimesFM loads via the HuggingFace transformers integration (TimesFmModelForPrediction, not the timesfm pip package — stays on Colab's numpy-2 stack); embeddings = last_hidden_state mean-pooled over patches, never the forecast head. Context L=64 = exactly 2 patches (patch_len 32).

Targets (from RAW target windows, H=16): future_return = Σ close log-ret; future_volatility = std close log-ret; direction = sign(Σ). All encoders receive the SAME per-sample-normalized context (re-normalized in the collector to match PriceWindowDataset(normalize=True)).

Analysis & Findings

1. TimesFM (this variant) does not clear the ceiling

RAW TimesFM pooled features are anti-informative for volatility on val (−0.134) despite a healthy train R² (+0.246) — a textbook probe-overfit signature at D=1280, and even maximal in-grid regularization (α=1e4) cannot pull val above zero. The supposed ceiling is the worst of the four on both regression targets. There is a real confound — TimesFM was fed only the close channel (variant="close") while raw-input saw all six — so this does not fully exonerate a multivariate TimesFM. But two things are already clear: (a) the close-channel pretrained representation does not linearly expose volatility better than a random projection, and (b) it overfits badly through a 2-patch pool. That is enough to refuse the pivot on current evidence.

2. The dominant finding: trivial moments beat everything on volatility

raw-input's +0.333 vol R² (val ≥ train, α mid-grid — a robust, non-overfit fit) is ~3.4× the random floor and the only number in the whole study that represents strong, reliable downstream signal. Volatility clusters, so a crude summary of recent context predicts future volatility almost linearly. Every learned/pretrained representation — random, trained, and TimesFM — underperforms four moments per channel.

Why the encoders can't match it — a likely input-pipeline flaw. The collector normalizes each context window per-channel by its own mean/std, so by construction std(normalized) ≈ 1 for every sample — the absolute volatility level (the single best predictor of future volatility) is normalized away before the encoder sees it. raw-input still scores +0.333, most plausibly via the residual that leaks through the σ + 1e-6 floor (std/(std+ε) is monotone in raw σ) and the per-channel "last" values. If that is right, the encoders receive the same leaked residue but fail to use it, while a linear probe on explicit moments extracts it directly. Implication: for a volatility target, causal per-sample scale normalization may be removing the very signal — the encoder needs access to scale (e.g. realized vol as an explicit feature, or don't normalize scale away).

3. Return is ~unpredictable; direction signal is weak and learned reps don't help

All future_return val R² are ≤ +0.012 (mostly negative) — consistent with an H=16 return being close to a martingale from price alone. On direction, only the untrained random projection clears the majority baseline by >2·SE (0.571 vs 0.528); trained z_price (0.535) and TimesFM (0.539) sit on chance. A random projection out-decoding a pretrained foundation model on direction is another statement that, for these targets at this horizon, representation richness is not the binding constraint.

Thought Process & Debugging Notes

First check on a −0.134 ceiling was "is the probe broken?" — ruled out the same way as Stage 1: the identical harness produces sensible numbers for the other three encoders (raw +0.333, untrained +0.097), so the harness is fine and TimesFM genuinely overfits. The α-pinning told the rest of the story: vol/return pinned at the grid max (under-regularized, overfit) and direction at the grid min (over-regularized, underfit) — a 1280-dim representation straining the probe at both ends. Widening the grid was tempting but pointless: a vol fit already at α=1e4 and still −0.134 will not cross zero with more L2.

The instinct to read the automated "INTERMEDIATE: beats floor" at face value was the trap — the verdict string was buggy (C < Fl was never above the floor). The raw numbers, not the printed label, are load-bearing; the label is now fixed. The bigger discipline lesson: the experiment was framed entirely around the ceiling (TimesFM), and the decisive result came from the control (raw-input). Always include the trivial baseline — here it inverted the conclusion from "which backbone?" to "the learned approach is losing to four moments".

Lessons Learned

  • The raw-input baseline is the most important encoder in the study. It reframed the bar from the +0.097 random floor to +0.33. Any learned model must beat the trivial baseline, not just a random projection.
  • A pretrained backbone is not automatically a richer probe target. RAW close-channel TimesFM overfit to a negative val R² — high capacity + a coarse 2-patch pool + per-sample-normalized inputs can be worse than 24 hand-crafted features.
  • Normalization can delete the target signal. Causal per-window scale normalization removes absolute volatility level — fine for return/direction, likely harmful for a volatility target. Match the normalization to the target.
  • Boundary-pinning at BOTH ends flags a capacity/regularization mismatch, not just a narrow grid. TimesFM wanting α→max on regression and α→min on direction is the D=1280 probe straining; more grid won't fix a structural overfit.
  • Trust the numbers over the auto-verdict. The "beats floor" string was wrong; the table was right. Verdict logic needs an explicit C < floor branch.

Next Steps

  1. HOLD the TimesFM pivot. Do not spend training units on a frozen-TimesFM JEPA loop — the ceiling probe does not clear the floor.
  2. Confirm what carries raw-input's +0.333 (forward passes only). Probe raw-input on UN-normalized context, and a single feature = raw context std, to test the "normalization removes scale" hypothesis. If raw σ alone reproduces ~+0.33, the input pipeline — not the backbone — is the lever for the volatility target.
  3. Only if (2) is inconclusive, run TimesFM variant="concat6" (all six channels concatenated) to close the channel confound. Lower priority: the moment baseline already shows representation richness is not the binding constraint here.
  4. Re-bar the project. The Stage 1 floor is no longer the reference for volatility — +0.33 val R² (raw moments) is. Any learned/JEPA representation must beat the trivial baseline to justify training. Re-examine whether volatility is the right target to chase with representation learning, or whether the value is in harder targets (direction with conditioning) or in adding options/futures/text conditioning where moments fail.
